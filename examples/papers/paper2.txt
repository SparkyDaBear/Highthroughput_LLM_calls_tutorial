TITLE: Atomic Force Microscopy Study of Cell Mechanics in Epithelial Tissues: How Mechanical Stress Regulates Cell Differentiation

ABSTRACT:
Cellular mechanics play a fundamental role in tissue development, homeostasis, and disease. While biochemical signaling pathways have been extensively studied, the mechanical aspects of cell-cell communication remain poorly understood. This study employs atomic force microscopy (AFM) to measure mechanical properties of individual epithelial cells and investigate how mechanical stress influences cell differentiation. We applied controlled mechanical stimulation to cultured epithelial cells and monitored changes in gene expression, cytoskeletal organization, and cell stiffness. Our results demonstrate that cells increase their stiffness in response to static mechanical stress and that this mechanical adaptation correlates with expression of tight junction proteins and increased barrier function. Dynamic mechanical stimulation (mimicking physiological conditions) enhanced cell migration capacity compared to static conditions. Analysis of cytoskeletal protein reorganization reveals that mechanotransduction occurs through YAP/TAZ pathway activation, leading to altered transcriptional programs. These findings have important implications for understanding tissue healing, fibrosis development, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in cancer progression.

INTRODUCTION:
The mechanical properties of cells and tissues fundamentally influence their biological functions. Epithelial tissues form physiological barriers and must balance structural integrity with dynamic responsiveness. While the role of mechanical forces in tissue development has long been recognized, quantitative characterization of how individual cells sense and respond to mechanical stimuli remains incomplete. Classical approaches emphasizing biochemical signaling have overlooked the direct transduction of mechanical signals into cellular responses.

Recent evidence suggests that cells respond to mechanical stress through mechanotransduction pathways involving proteins such as integrins, YAP/TAZ transcription factors, and components of the focal adhesion complex. However, most studies examine averaged population responses, and the relationship between applied mechanical stress, cellular stiffness changes, and functional differentiation remains unclear. AFM provides unique capabilities for measuring single-cell mechanics while simultaneously perturbing cellular stress states.

METHODS:
We cultured Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) epithelial cells on tissue culture plastic and glass substrates with varying elasticity (1-1000 kPa). Cell stiffness was measured using AFM force spectroscopy, acquiring force-distance curves at 64 locations per cell from 20-30 cells per condition. Applied forces ranged from 1-10 nanoNewtons, covering physiological ranges. Mechanical stimulation involved either constant stress application (static, 24 hours) or oscillatory stress (dynamic, 1 Hz, 24 hours).

Following mechanical stimulation, we assessed: (1) cell stiffness via Young's modulus calculation from force curves, (2) gene expression via quantitative RT-PCR for tight junction (ZO-1, Occludin) and mesenchymal markers (Vimentin, N-cadherin), (3) protein localization via immunofluorescence, and (4) migration capacity via transwell assays.

RESULTS:
Baseline cell stiffness measurements showed mean Young's modulus of 1.2 ± 0.4 kPa for MDCK cells, consistent with prior literature. Following 24-hour static mechanical stress (applied force: 5 nanoNewtons), cell stiffness increased to 2.1 ± 0.6 kPa (p < 0.001, 75% increase). This stiffening correlated with 4.2-fold increase in ZO-1 expression and 2.8-fold increase in Occludin expression, indicating enhanced tight junction protein synthesis. Barrier function assessed by TEER measurements increased from 800 to 1200 ohms·cm², representing a 50% enhancement.

In contrast, cells exposed to dynamic mechanical stress (1 Hz oscillatory loading) showed modest stiffness increase (1.6 ± 0.5 kPa) but dramatically enhanced migration (2.1-fold increase in transwell migration, p < 0.01). These cells showed relatively lower tight junction protein expression and higher expression of mesenchymal markers (Vimentin: 3.5-fold increase, p < 0.05), suggesting transition toward migratory phenotype.

Immunofluorescence revealed that YAP/TAZ localization shifted from diffuse cytoplasmic distribution in control cells to robust nuclear localization in mechanically stimulated cells (nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio increased from 0.3 to 2.1). This subcellular redistribution correlated with increased expression of YAP/TAZ target genes including CTGF and Ankrd1. Pharmacological inhibition of YAP/TAZ abolished the mechanical stress-induced changes in gene expression, confirming this pathway's necessity for the observed responses.

DISCUSSION:
These results establish a direct link between applied mechanical stress, cellular stiffness adaptation, and transcriptional programming in epithelial cells. Static stress appears to activate epithelial differentiation programs, whereas dynamic stress promotes migratory phenotypes—a distinction with important implications for tissue engineering and wound healing applications. The YAP/TAZ pathway emerges as a central hub converting mechanical signals to biological responses.

The dramatic correlation between cell stiffness changes and functional outcomes suggests that mechanical properties themselves may serve as biomarkers of cell state. Notably, the stress levels applied (1-10 nanoNewtons) fall within the range observed during normal tissue function, suggesting these responses are physiologically relevant.

CONCLUSION:
Epithelial cells employ mechanotransduction mechanisms to couple external mechanical stress to transcriptional and phenotypic changes. Static mechanical loading enhances barrier function through YAP/TAZ-mediated expression of tight junction proteins and increased cellular stiffness. Dynamic loading promotes migration through activation of EMT-related transcriptional programs. These findings provide mechanistic understanding of how epithelia adapt to changing mechanical environments, with applications to wound healing, fibrosis prevention, and cancer metastasis prevention.
